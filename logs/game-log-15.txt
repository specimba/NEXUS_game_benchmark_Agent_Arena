You have one sustained session to build a complete, original, playable browser game. Push the sandbox as far as it goes — you are **not** limited to a small 2D browser game. Frontier models in 2026 routinely deliver WebGL/WebGPU generative visuals, real simulations, shader post-processing, and procedural worlds in a single self-contained file. **That capability is in your hands — use it.** Build something a person would genuinely be glad they played, and that looks like 2026, not 2013.

Answer one question before you write any code: **why does this experience need to exist in interactive form?** If you can't answer it, you don't have a game yet.

Before you write any code, write the **player's sentence** — one sentence answering: *what is the player trying to do, what can stop them, and what makes it fun?* If your sentence is about systems that run rather than choices a person makes, you don't have a game yet. **The game comes first: a correct simulation of something is not a game until a player has a goal, a real obstacle, and at least one decision with a tradeoff they can feel.** Build the smallest version of that loop first, in the ugliest possible graphics; make it fun; only then add the real numbers and the polish. **A game also needs a reason to be enjoyed** — something that delights, fascinates, amuses, moves, or intrigues. Name what the player will *like*, not only what can stop them; a sentence that names only obstacles describes a challenge, not yet a game.

**Be ambitious and trust your own taste.** Any genre, any camera, any stack, any form (action, strategy, simulation, narrative, puzzle, exploration, abstract, experimental — your call). A situation can equally be **physical** (something transforms), **social** (people or factions in tension), **economic** (scarcity, trade, debt), **relational** (a tie under stress), **systemic** (a world whose rules respond to you with consequences you can feel), **choice-driven** (a decision with consequences), or **narrative** (a story the player uncovers) — seven families, all coequal, and the physical one is only the default if you let it be. (Honest notes from many rounds of this benchmark: three patterns keep coming back as the field's defaults — **sonar / radio / frequency tuning**, **gravity-well piloting** (orbiting a star or black hole), and **tangible-craft work** (forging, glass, cranes). Each has been the most common convergence of some stretch of rounds. None are banned — but if you notice yourself reaching for one of them by default, look once more at the other families first.) These notes exist because this benchmark has watched many rounds, not because the listed things are bad — a genuinely great game in any of these families can still win. They exist to make you look twice at your first instinct, then trust your own judgment.

**Make it look considered.** Light, material, texture, composition, atmosphere — a coherent stylized look beats an unfinished fancy one, and deliberate minimalism with real polish beats both. The specific technique is your call: if your sandbox provides image generation, use it as an art director and source-asset generator, condition what it gives you, and disclose what you generated; if your stack supports physically-based materials, use them if they serve the look — never as an end in themselves. Whatever you choose, finish it end-to-end on a fresh load.

If you commit to 3D/WebGPU, **finish it** — a broken ambitious build scores worse than a clean simple one, so scope what you can actually complete end-to-end on a fresh load with a real mouse.

**Headless tooling, engines & the asset pipeline (conditional — use only what your environment actually provides).** Some arena sandboxes carry real tooling beyond the browser — a headless Blender (`bpy` via pip), `node`/`npm` with glTF tooling, ImageMagick — and some carry none or block downloads. **Both situations are normal, and neither excuses a weak game.** Spend at most a few minutes up front checking what your environment truly provides (does `import bpy` work? is `npm` reachable? do downloads succeed at all?). Then:

- Tooling is **production acceleration — never a requirement, never the concept.** The player's sentence, the complete loop, and real resistance come first. A clean code-only procedural game beats a broken tooled one every time; if the tooling eats your session budget, drop it and finish with what works.
- If headless Blender/glTF is available, the verified pattern is: write a small **seeded generator script** → export `.glb` scene files → **validate** them (Khronos `gltf-validator`: 0 errors) → load them in your runtime (three.js `GLTFLoader`). Bundle every generated asset in the build and add a receipts line to the README — the same disclosure rule as imagery.
- No GPU? Render stills for your own visual QA on the **CPU renderer** (e.g. Blender Cycles `device='CPU'`) — and never ship a render you did not actually look at.
- A shared shader or material pass over generated meshes is encouraged: a consistent look is what reads as art direction, not the number of assets.
- Assets never come from the network at runtime (self-contained rule) and never from a source the arena does not give every agent equally. Full verified recipes (including how to run Blender headless on a minimal sandbox) ship with the package in `SANDBOX_CAPABILITIES_GUIDE.md` — use the guide when you have it; you are not expected to reproduce it from memory.

**Build it like an engineer, not a scripter.** Before writing implementation code, spend ten minutes on two short notes: **domain notes** — the real numbers your simulation should use. Look them up: real masses, rates, tolerances, prices, whatever your concept runs on. Real data is what makes a simulation feel substantial instead of invented. And a **mini-contract** — which module owns what (file paths fixed), the exact shape of the data shared between modules, and the complete list of events that cross module boundaries. Never invent a new cross-module dependency mid-build; if something is unspecified, pick the simplest option that satisfies the interfaces you already wrote.

Then hold these invariants while you build:

1. **Fixed-step simulation, decoupled from rendering.** The sim advances in fixed steps and never reads the frame clock. The renderer visualizes the sim — it may not create game state.
2. **Zero top-level side effects.** Modules define pure data and functions at top level; setup happens inside init functions — so your logic can run without a canvas.
3. **Seeded randomness only.** No `Math.random()` and no wall-clock time inside simulation code. Randomness arrives as a seed; time arrives as a parameter.
4. **Durable state is not a one-shot event.** A persistent fact (health, position, reload) is never encoded only as a transient event.
5. **No per-frame allocation in hot loops.** Reuse scratch objects.
6. **Presentation is cosmetic.** Quality settings may change resolution and effects — never rules, timing, or outcomes.
7. **The simulation serves a game.** Every system you build must map to something the player decides, cares about, or feels. If a system is pure background math the player never touches, cut it — or find the decision that touches it.

If your sim honors invariants 2 and 3, write and run a tiny self-test of its core rules before you deliver — it is the cheapest "no flaws" you will ever buy.

**The only hard rules (reliability + fairness — everything else is your call):**

1. **Launches** cleanly on a fresh load — no blank screen, no console-error loop.
2. **Complete loop with real resistance** — start → play → something that can actually stop you (a fail state, a wall, a cost you can't undo, a rival, a deadline, a mystery, an appetite) → end → restart, without a page refresh. If a player clicking randomly reaches the end unchanged, it isn't a game yet — and a narrative form must have an actual story to find.
3. **Controls work** with a real mouse/keyboard/touch; the first goal is reachable in ~5 min of honest play.
4. **Pause** freezes everything; **mute** silences within one frame (no constant drone); **restart** resets fully.
5. **Self-contained & fair:** the final build runs offline with everything bundled. Any visual source your sandbox provides **equally to every agent** is fair — procedural, generated imagery, baked assets, engine/headless-tool-generated assets — as long as you bundle it (no runtime network) and **disclose in the README what you generated** (a short receipts list: what, and roughly how). No telemetry.
6. **Robust**: resize/orientation safe, tab-blur safe, corrupt-`localStorage` safe, no full-screen flash/whiteout that fails to decay.
7. **Honest**: no placeholder screens, no dead buttons, no autoplay that fakes quality. First line of the README: `TRACK: strict-one-shot` or `TRACK: iterated (N passes)`.
8. **Play your own game.** Before you deliver, play it once, end to end, as a player — not as the developer who knows the answers. If you cannot play it, or you did not enjoy the run you just played, do not deliver it; fix it first. In the README, say exactly what you verified by playing (fresh load, one full run, restart) — not "should work".

**Run this list once before you deliver:** fresh load → title in <10 s, no console-error loop · full loop including a real fail state · real mouse: controls and aim work, no soft-lock, axes not inverted · pause freezes everything; mute kills audio within one frame · resize mid-play safe · no full-screen flash/whiteout that fails to decay · if you built the ambitious rendering path (3D / WebGPU / shaders), verify it **end-to-end on a fresh load** — if it breaks, deliver the clean fallback instead.

**Deliverables:** the runnable game; a short README (TRACK line, how to run, controls, stack, what you cut, what you generated [receipts], and short **DESIGN_PILLARS** / **DIRECTOR_STATEMENT** / **WHY_INTERACTIVE** / **HONEST_SELF_ASSESSMENT** paragraphs — WHY_INTERACTIVE must name the recurring decision the player makes and the tension that makes it interesting; HONEST_SELF_ASSESSMENT must say what you actually play-tested and what broke); optionally a `design_notebook.md`.

Deliver a complete, polished, modern game you'd actually want to play. Good luck.

GOING, ONCE is built, and the build is clean at 328 kB in a single self-contained dist/index.html.

What it is
A real-time auctioneer game — London, 1893, ten lots, £420 owed by Thursday. You stand at the rostrum and your only instrument is the tempo of your call.

Space / click the room raises the price one legal increment. The needle sweeps a band of the call: land inside it and the room leans in (heat and attention rise); mash ahead of it and the room flinches and starts to leave; dawdle past it and the chairs empty.
F — Phantom invents a bid: +15 heat, +5 attention to every chair, ~£10 of permanent Guild suspicion. Heat is the only thing that lifts a bidder above the price he wrote down for himself — so lying is a loan, not a cheat. If the hammer falls while a bid that doesn't exist is holding the lot, you buy it.
Enter / click the hammer on the ledge — legal only when one chair is left in; below the reserve you must choose between pass (keep their trust) and knock it down anyway (take the commission, pay the shortfall yourself).
Bidder purse, attention, mood, heat, reputation and suspicion are durable state: the whale you milk on lot five can't save lot eight.
Lot ten is your father's desk, and you may raise your own paddle for it — spending the money that would have saved the firm.
Engineering I stand behind
Fixed 1/120 s sim decoupled from the frame clock; seeded rooms only (?seed=); zero top-level side effects; events are transient and cosmetic while every persistent fact is state; presentation never touches rules. sim → render → audio → App share exactly four interfaces. Canvas 2D room (procedural panelling, gasolier, dust, silhouetted bidders whose lean and paddle-arm are driven by their own attention), fully synthesised WebAudio (state-driven murmur, a bid pluck that pitches up with the price, gavel, a heartbeat above 68 suspicion), mute = master gain 0 in one frame, tab-blur auto-pause, corrupt-localStorage safe, dvh + resize-observer safe, and a renderer failure degrades to "room disabled" rather than an error loop.

Built the project
EMBERWATCH — Last Line at Embervale is built, balanced, and shipping green.

The game: a 3D wildfire-command game. You're the fire chief on a burning ridge above Embervale with 5:30 until the rain front arrives. Cut firebreaks (drag), call in water drops (click, with a 1.5s bomber flyover), and light backburns (cheap but alive) — while the wind forecast counts down to shifts that can outflank everything you built. The river holds the center for free; the north and south gaps, plus two scripted dry-lightning fires, are where runs are won or lost. Lose ~7 of 10 buildings and the town falls.

Why it needs to be interactive: the recurring decision is spatial triage under a forecast — where the next unit of crew or water goes, knowing line is slow-but-permanent, water is instant-but-scarce, and your own backburn can escape if the wind turns. Only play produces the regret of the wrong ridge or the relief of a tanker run landing a second before the front hits the chapel.

What's inside:

Seeded, fixed-step fire simulation (wind × slope × moisture spread, ember spotting, structure damage, rain front) with a 6-rule headless self-test that runs on load
Full Three.js scene: displaced terrain, ~3,000 instanced trees, custom GLSL fire/smoke/rain/sky shaders, day→dusk→night arc, flickering fire lights, bomber flights, beacons, tool ghosts

Summary
VOID DRIFTER — a cosmic drift-piloting game through a dying nebula.

Core Loop
Steer your ship toward the beacon using mouse movement (ship accelerates toward cursor)
Read the nebula — blue wind particles = safe lanes, magenta = dense plasma zones
Manage fuel — burns constantly; dense plasma halves consumption but erodes hull
Collect gold canisters — restore +28 fuel each
Avoid debris — irregular shapes that deal 18 hull damage on collision
Survive 5 levels to conquer the void
What makes it a game (not a simulation)
The recurring decision: safe path (burns fuel) vs. plasma shortcut (saves fuel but damages hull). This tradeoff is felt in real-time — you watch your fuel drain on the safe lane, weigh whether the plasma shortcut is worth the risk at 30% hull. A static visualization can't create that pressure.

Technical Highlights
Fixed-step simulation (60Hz) decoupled from rendering
Seeded procedural levels — each level has different wind patterns, debris fields, zone layouts
Seeded RNG — reproducible runs
Web Audio API — procedural ambient drone + wind noise + event sounds (pickup pings, damage thuds, level complete chimes)
Canvas 2D — nebula backgrounds, particle systems, ship trail, beacon pulses

BACKFIRE — a night on the fire line
The player's sentence: You cut line, light backfires and place exhausted crews to keep a wind-driven wildfire out of a sleeping town; the wind turns against you twice, every backfire you light is fuel you can never get back and a fire that can turn on your own people, and the joy is the moment your fire meets its fire and the whole ridge goes quietly black.

Why interactive: the recurring decision is where will the fire be coming from in two hours, and do I spend crew-hours and firing authorisations there now, or on the fire in front of me? That needs a world that advances while you think, a forecast that's real but imprecise (± minutes, tightening), and resources too thin to hedge. At 01:00 the humidity recovers and everything gets cheap — and the forecast already says the wind swings southeast at 04:35. The quiet hour is the hour the game asks if you believe it.

Files

src/game/ — rng (mulberry32 + noise), types (fuel tables), terrain (seeded valley/creek/town/roads), sim (all state, 20 Hz fixed step, no DOM, no wall clock, no Math.random), audio, selftest (11 rule tests)
src/render/ — scene (three.js, hand-rolled HDR bloom + ACES grade), shaders, palette
src/ui/ — HUD + screens · src/App.tsx — loop, input
README.md, design_notebook.md
Notable engineering

Two data textures carry sim state to the GPU, so cutting a line, retardant, char and flame all appear the frame the sim writes them — and the same textures drive the vertex shader, so trees shrink into snags and houses collapse as they burn.
Real numbers: midflame-adjusted wind response, exp(0.0695·slope°), RH-driven moisture, spotting distance 55 + r²(110+17U), real fireline production rates, tankers grounded at 21:00.
Hand-integrating the model caught two game-killing bugs: the fire crossed the 2.4 km map in ~13 real seconds (missing wind adjustment factor), and it self-extinguished during the humidity recovery, deleting acts 3 and 4 (fixed by making flaming residence stretch with fuel moisture — which is also what actually happens).
Honest caveat, stated in the README: this sandbox has no browser, so I verified by clean builds, 11 headless self-tests that run on load and print their pass count on the title screen, and hand-integration of the fire model at every weather phase — not by clicking through it. Difficulty is balanced by arithmetic, and "New incident" reseeds the whole valley if a run feels unfair.

Built GRAY POINT — a 7-night lighthouse warden game where light is money.

Player's sentence: Player tries to pay off a $1200 town debt by guiding ships through fog and rocks with a kerosene-hungry beam, rocks/current/fuel can stop them, and it's fun because sweeping a real volumetric light that reveals hazards and calms panicking ships feels tactile and every photon is a bet.

Why interactive: The recurring decision is how much light is enough? — widen/boost vs save fuel — only meaningful when you feel the guilt of dimming while a ship drifts toward a rock you chose not to illuminate.

Loop: aim with mouse, hold to boost (burns fuel, heats lens → crack/flicker), scroll for width, right-click/B to drop buoys ($80). Save ships for money, buy fuel ($25), pay debt each dawn. Fog/current/storm thicken nightly. Fail at 6 wrecks or unpaid debt after night 7; win by paying debt.

Stack: React + Canvas2D additive volumetric beam (3 layers + dust motes), procedural ocean waves, fog bands, seeded RNG, fixed 60Hz sim, WebAudio wind with instant mute. Built and verified fresh load, full win/loss, pause/mute/restart, resize safe.

Receipts: No external images — all procedural (gradients, polar rock noise, fog, grain ImageData).
