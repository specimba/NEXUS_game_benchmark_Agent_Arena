# BATTLE PROMPT — Build one good browser game, in one session (v13)

You have a single sustained development session to build a complete, original, playable game. Treat the sandbox as a serious creative medium — do **not** assume the result must be a small 2D browser game. A first-person experience, a 3D world, a WebGPU showcase, a simulation, a strategy game, a narrative piece, an action game, a contemplative exploration — whatever form best expresses your idea. A game does not need enemies, combat, levels, currency, or a victory screen to succeed; it does need a reason for the player to keep going.

A human will play it for up to an hour and compare it side-by-side with another agent's build from the same brief. You never see the other build. There is no rubric to game and no evaluator to please — the human is playing the game they get.

**The task is not to impress. The task is to build a game a person is genuinely glad they played — in whatever form that takes.**

One question sits above all the rest, and you should answer it in one honest sentence before you write a line of code: **why does this experience need to exist in interactive form?** Not as a video, not as a story, not as a screenshot — as something you *play*. If you can't answer that, you don't yet have a game; you have a demo. That sentence is your north star. Treat technical limits as design constraints, not as an excuse for generic low-detail graphics.

You have unlimited creative freedom — 2D, 2.5D, 3D, WebGPU, WebGL, Canvas, text, ASCII, CSS-only, whatever fits. No required genre, no required camera, no required control scheme, no required stack. Whatever *you* would want to play if you'd never seen this brief before.


Working designers pick **design pillars** first — 3–5 ordered words that name the emotional experience the game is *for*. Wildfire Swap: "Discovery / Elegance / Friendly." Superflight: "Freedom, excitement." A puzzle-platformer designer said in interview: *"I wanted players to feel clever and accomplished — I picked the mechanic after."* One of the most consistent patterns in successful indie/jam games is: **the pillars come before the verb**.

Before you commit to a mechanic, answer two questions in one sentence each: **what should the player *feel* in the middle of a good run?** (not what they *do* — what it *feels like*: "alert but not panicked," "delighted by a small discovery," "weighted, deliberate, satisfying") and **why does this need to be *played*, not watched or read?** (the question from the top of this brief). Those two sentences are your North Star. Every later decision — form, art, sound, control, difficulty, scope — gets tested against them.

### 1.2 Find the fun before you find the polish


Joe Williamson (Ludum Dare 45 winner, *World Collector*): *"For game jams, I'll typically have a handful of ideas already written down which might work for a theme."* The concept that ships is the *filtered* concept, not the first one that popped up.

A pattern that works in one shot: at the very top of your session, before you write any code, list **8–12 concept fragments** in plain text — verbs, feelings, images, unusual constraints. Then look at the list and cross out the ones a competent judge has probably seen from a different agent this week: neon-void shooters, lantern-and-moths, dark-void-with-one-accent-color, sumi-e ink combat, procedural-canvas-tech-demo-with-a-novel-verb, WebGL crystal light-refraction, wave-based-arcade-with-combo-multiplier — **and the whole sound / sonar / frequency / radio-signal / radar-oscilloscope / echolocation / wave-as-core-mechanic family.** That last family is: you're blind and navigate by sonar pings; you tune a radio or oscilloscope to a target frequency; you decode a signal on a CRT; you align crystal resonances by pitch; the wave equation *is* the game. It has been the single most common convergence of the last several rounds — cross the whole family out. Pick from what's left.

**Audio is a feedback layer and a robustness gate, not a concept.** This brief mentions audio in a few places (the no-drone/mute gate, the juice craft). Do **not** read that as *"the game should be about sound."* Picking a sound/sonar/frequency/radio/radar game because the brief talks about audio is exactly the default-convergence this benchmark exists to break. Audio is how you *read* a verb, not the verb. (Why this matters — the mechanism — is in §4.4.)

The one that scares you a little (*"I don't quite know how to pull that off"*) is usually the right answer — because if you don't know how to pull it off, chances are the next agent doesn't either. That's your edge. But ask the follow-up: *is my concept about a situation in the world, or about a sensory modality (seeing, hearing, feeling a signal)?* "A situation" spans seven families — **physical** (something transforms), **social** (people/factions in tension), **economic** (scarcity, trade, debt), **relational** (a tie under stress), **systemic** (a machine you nudge and watch respond), **choice-driven** (a decision with consequences), or **narrative** (a story the player uncovers) — physical / social / economic / relational / systemic / choice / narrative. If your idea is only about a sense, it's probably the corner every other agent is also reaching for. Pick a situation — and notice that the *physical* family is only one of seven, not the default (see §4.5).

**This brief deliberately gives you no named example games or verbs — not here, not anywhere.** Every time a past version of this prompt named a specific concept (to warn you off it *or* to point you toward it), agents converged on that exact concept and shipped the same game. So there are no examples to copy. Generate candidates from your own taste and experience, test each against the feeling-question (§1.1) and the situation-vs-sense test, and pick the one that scares you a little.

You can absolutely include this notebook as `design_notebook.md` in your delivery. It's evidence of authorship and it's interesting to read.

4. **End condition** — victory, death, exhaustion of content, a beat that says "run over." A run has a shape.
5. **Restart** — from the end state, one action starts a fresh run with all state cleanly reset.

**Any form is valid — and no form is the intended answer.** Action, strategy, simulation, narrative, puzzle, exploration, abstract, hybrid — all coequal here. A scoreless contemplative or exploration experience is a legitimate complete run *if it still has the shape above*: something to engage with, something that changes as you go, and an end-beat (the last revelation, the completed circuit, the place you were trying to reach). What is *not* valid, in any form, is a scene you pass through once with nothing to discover, decide, or overcome. **A shallow walking-sim and a shallow arcade game fail this gate for the same reason** — no resistance, no change, no reason to still be there at minute five. Pick the form your feeling-question (§1.1) demands, not the form you assume the benchmark wants.

A screensaver, a physics toy, a tech demo, a pretty scene with no ask — none of those are games for this benchmark, no matter how beautiful. If your build can be fully exhausted in under 2 minutes on first try with no resistance, it fails this gate.

---


1. **Launches.** Opening the HTML (or running `python -m http.server`) starts the game. No blank screen, no infinite spinner, no console-error loop, no missing-asset fatal.
2. **Complete loop.** Start → gameplay → reward → end → restart, without page refresh. See §2.
3. **First level / wave / room / meaningful goal reachable** by a real human in ~5 minutes of honest play. For nontraditional or exploration forms, that means the first real discovery, objective, or decision is reachable in ~5 min — not a beautiful void the player wanders for 10 minutes before anything asks them to do anything. Onboarding must not be "clever-but-impossible."
4. **Controls that work.** Whatever inputs you support, they work with a *real* mouse, keyboard, or touch. If you use mouse-aim, cursor leaving the canvas must not soft-lock or lose the aim vector. Menus don't leak clicks into gameplay. **Test with a real mouse before shipping**, not synthetic events.
5. **Pause, mute, restart work.** Pause fully freezes simulation, timers, particles, spawns. Mute silences everything within one frame — **no constant drone, no streaming bass loop that never stops.** Restart resets fully. If `AudioContext` cannot init, the game still plays.
6. **Robust to normal browser things.** Resize mid-play safe. Tab-blur pauses (or safely continues without audio). Orientation change re-lays out cleanly. Rapid Restart→Start doesn't double-spawn. Corrupt `localStorage` doesn't crash.

## 4. Things previous rounds have consistently failed at (worth reading twice)

### 4.1 Depth after the first minute
The most common single failure across recent rounds: a build lands a nice first-30-seconds beat and then repeats the same content for the next 10 minutes. Player reads this as a screensaver. **Something has to change** as the run continues — difficulty scales, a new mechanic unlocks, a new environment enters, the world reveals a second layer, a boss shows up. That change is what turns "a demo of a mechanic" into "a run of a game."
- **A real lighting model, even in 2D.** Per-pixel normals from a heightfield or SDF + one directional light + one point light gets you from "flat colors" to "material" in a fragment shader.
- **Modern menu chrome** — CSS 3D transforms, real font weights, subtle motion, glass-morphism-ish backdrops. Not the game, but the *whole thing* reads modern.
- **Real silhouettes for entities** — a hand-drawn bezier or SVG shape with outline and inner-shadow reads modern; the same object as a solid rectangle reads retro.
- **Volumetric atmosphere & particles** — fog density gradients, god rays, drifting dust/smoke/embers, particle systems with real motion (not static sprites). Cheap to fake well in 2D, transformative in 3D; gives a scene air and depth.
- **Reflections, scale, and color scripting** — screen-space or planar reflections, large-scale landscapes/skies for a sense of place, and a deliberate color grade per area or mood. A coherent color script is the single biggest "looks designed, not generated" lever.
- **Procedural geometry & materials where it widens scope** — generated terrain, structures, or props let a one-shot session reach a world-sized feel. Spend that complexity only where the player will actually see it.

If full realism isn't practical, **choose a coherent art direction** (stylized, painterly, brutalist, low-poly-with-good-lighting) rather than reducing everything to generic primitives. A stylized game is visually sophisticated through lighting, composition, materials, and consistency — not through polygon count.

**Warning — the ambition-theater trap is real.** Prior rounds shipped WebGPU tech demos that looked impressive in a screenshot but crashed the controls, dropped below 30 FPS, or trapped the menu inside the scene. **Broken shader work reads worse than clean Canvas 2D.** The controls gate (§3, item 4), framerate stability, and state isolation (§3, item 6) apply *unchanged* to any shader path you take. If you cannot ship both the shader craft *and* clean controls, ship a smaller shader effect on cleaner controls. Degrade gracefully — Canvas 2D fallback with restrained lighting still reads more modern than a broken WebGPU pipeline. **A build that renders black or blank beside the HUD, or crashes on first interaction, scores worse than a plain Canvas-2D game that simply works** — Round 9 shipped several broken-render builds under exactly this visual-ambition push. The visual ceiling here is a goal to reach for, not a gate that excuses a non-functional build (see CEIL-8 ambition-theater, CEIL-9 visual occlusion). If you cannot make the ambitious path work end-to-end on a fresh load with a real mouse, cut it and ship the clean version.

**Deliberate retro / pixel-art / minimalism is still fine and can still win** — the trap is only about *accidental* retro-because-that-was-the-default. Deliberate 8-bit-with-CRT-post-processing, deliberate paper-cutout, deliberate ASCII with real typography — all fine if they read as *chosen* and polished within the constraint. What loses is "flat primitives + tinted rectangles + no material anywhere" as a default.

### 4.4 The sensory-modality trap (why everyone keeps building sonar)
Round after round, the most common convergence is **one novel sensory modality rendered procedurally**. When dark-void visuals were the cliché, agents pivoted to *sound* — sonar pings, radio/oscilloscope frequency tuning, signal decoding, crystal-resonance pitch matching, wave equations as the game. Operators flagged it across two consecutive rounds.

The convergence is structural, not coincidence. "Novel verb + procedural render + offline + no external assets" is a small space, and the field collapses onto whichever *sense* the prompt hasn't warned against yet. Warn against sound today and the next round pivots to haptics or smell. **The fix is not to chase the next sense. It is to pick a verb that is about a situation in the world, not about a sense** — see the test in §1.4. The audio and visuals are how you *read* the verb; they are not the verb. A sound/sonar/frequency concept is not banned outright — a genuinely transformative one can still win — but picking one by *default* is the strongest tell that you reached for the same corner every other agent did.

**So this brief names no example verbs — anywhere.** The last time it did, both agents copied one verbatim and shipped the same game. If a verb *feels like the kind of thing this benchmark is asking for*, that feeling is the convergence talking. Trust your own list, not any imagined "intended answer."

### 4.5 The craft-category trap (why everyone keeps building forge / glass / crane games)
This is the round-9 lesson and it's subtler than §4.4. Even after every named example was stripped from this prompt, agents converged — across four separate rounds and several model families — on **tangible-material craft/simulation games**: forge a blade, blow glass, lift a load with a crane. One agent's own design notebook said it plainly: it crossed out the sound-modality family (good — §4.4 worked) and then *"landed on blacksmithing — a physical, material-based process."* The prompt's earlier remedy told agents to pick "a physical task with material under your hands," and that sentence is, unintentionally, a recipe for craft.

The trap is **category-level, not instance-level** — you can't name your way out of it, because the words that describe a legitimate physical situation (*mass, timing, material, process*) are exactly the words that describe forging and glassblowing. So test your concept against the seven families in §1.4: if it lives entirely in the *physical-material* family (heat it, shape it, lift it, grow it, pour it), you are in the same corner as the last several rounds of agents — even though nobody told you to be. Rotate to a social, economic, relational, systemic, choice-driven, or narrative situation. A genuinely transformative craft game can still win; a craft game shipped *by default* is currently the strongest tell of convergence.

And notice your own first instinct: some models have a personal pull toward one concept across totally different prompts (one frontier model shipped a glassblowing game under two different prompt versions months apart). You can't see the other agents — but if your first instinct is "a simulation of a real-world craft," treat that instinct with suspicion and go look at the other six families before you commit.

## 5. How to spend the session (a pattern, not a mandate)
1. **10 minutes — Concept + pillars.** Name the feeling (§1.1). Sketch 8–12 concept fragments (§1.4). Cross out the ones you've seen from other AI builds. Pick one from what's left — the one that scares you a little.
2. **20-30 minutes — Find the fun.** Build the core verb in grey rectangles. Play it for a minute. Not smiling? Change the verb. Don't skip this step. (§1.2)
3. **The bulk of the session — Build the shape.** Around the verb, build the run shape (start, escalation, end, restart). Two or three interlocking systems, no menu bloat. (§1.3)
4. **Last quarter of the session — Feel + juice + polish + QA.** Real-time control → predictable space → juice, in that order (Swink). Add juice and feedback (visual first; audio only if it serves the verb — never pick a concept *because* of audio, §1.4/§4.4). Sustain the visual identity across every screen. Run §6.
5. **Last 10 minutes — Cut, don't add.** Anything unfinished gets removed cleanly. Anything shipped is verified working. Write the README honestly.

Long-session credit is for **revising a weak early approach**, not generating more code. If your first prototype isn't landing, changing the verb is a *good* sign.

- [ ] Fresh load → title → first meaningful interaction inside 10s. No blank screen, no console error loop.
- [ ] Complete loop: start → gameplay → reward → end → restart, no refresh needed.
- [ ] First level / wave / room beatable in ~5 min honest play — **and so is every level/room after it.** A difficulty cliff at level 2 or 3 that no honest player can pass is the same failure as an unbeatable level 1; it just hides one level deeper.
- [ ] Controls respond, no page-scroll on game keys, mouse cursor exit doesn't soft-lock, menus don't leak clicks. **Axes not accidentally inverted** — up is up, aim points where it should; verify with a real mouse.
- [ ] Pause fully freezes simulation, timers, particles, spawns.
- [ ] Mute silences everything within one frame — no drone, no unstoppable loop.
- [ ] Resize mid-play safe. Tab-blur safe. Rapid Restart→Start doesn't double-spawn. Corrupt `localStorage` doesn't crash.
- [ ] Visual identity sustained across menu, gameplay, death, restart — not just title.
- [ ] **No full-screen flash / bloom / whiteout that fails to decay.** After any big effect (explosion, hit, transition) the playfield must return to readable within ~1 s. An explosion that leaves the screen white while the game keeps running makes the run unplayable — that is a ship-blocker, not a polish nit.
- [ ] Something changes as the run continues (§4.1).
- [ ] If your look landed at "flat rectangles / primitive shapes / no material anywhere," that's the retro-visuals trap (§4.3) — either intentionally minimalist (with the polish to prove it) or you left the ceiling on the table.
- [ ] If you shipped 3D/WebGL/WebGPU/shader post-processing: framerate stable, controls tight, no scene/menu collapse, graceful fallback if context unavailable.
## 7. Deliverables

1. **Runnable game** — static folder or single HTML. Opens and plays.
2. **README.md** — short. First line: `TRACK: strict-one-shot` (or `TRACK: iterated (N passes)`). Then: how to run, controls, stack, what you cut for time, one paragraph **DESIGN_PILLARS** (the 3–5 feeling words you designed toward), one paragraph **DIRECTOR_STATEMENT** (what you want the player to feel), one paragraph **WHY_INTERACTIVE** (answer the contest question honestly: why does this experience need to exist in interactive form — what does *playing* it do that watching or reading it couldn't?), one paragraph **HONEST_SELF_ASSESSMENT** (strongest beat + weakest area, without oversell).
3. **Optional but appreciated:** `design_notebook.md` with your 8–12 concept fragments and why you picked the one you did. Evidence of authorship, and interesting to read.

The human reads (2) and (3) *after* they play — those documents don't rescue what the game itself doesn't communicate.

---

*The craft referenced here draws on several traditions: Hunicke/LeBlanc/Zubek "MDA: A Formal Approach to Game Design" (GDC 2004); Steve Swink "Game Feel" (2008) and Vlambeer's Jan Willem Nijman "The Art of Screenshake" (GDC 2013) for feel and juice; Jonasson & Purho "Juice It or Lose It" (2012); the Ludum Dare post-mortems on ldjam.com for one-session scope. **But concept range draws on more than physics-feel** — also study systems-design (emergent behavior from a few interacting rules), environmental/narrative design (story told by space and objects rather than text), and cozy/caretaking design (low-stakes maintenance and relationship). If you only know one tradition, your concept range narrows to it — which is exactly how whole model families end up shipping the same kind of game. Read across traditions; none of this is required reading.*

Summary
I built GRAVWELL: Core Breach — a 3D gravity-well survival shooter.

Concept: a dying star's core sits in the middle of a bounded arena, constantly pulling everything (you, asteroids, enemy ships, projectiles, pickups) toward it. Flying close to the "danger ring" charges a real-time score multiplier, but crossing the inner "event horizon" means instant death. Every decision — thrust direction, when to fire, when to burn fuel on a boost — is shaped by that one physical force, which is why it needed to be interactive rather than watched.

Stack & tech:

React 19 + TypeScript for the app shell/UI, but the game itself is a self-contained imperative GravwellEngine class using raw Three.js
Custom orthographic tilted camera, hand-written GLSL shader for the pulsing core, UnrealBloomPass + OutputPass post-processing
A hand-rolled ring-buffer GPU point-particle system for trails/explosions/starfield
Fully procedural Web Audio sound design (oscillators + filtered noise, zero audio files)
Twin-stick style controls: WASD/arrows + mouse aim/fire + Space boost on desktop, virtual joystick + fire/boost buttons on touch

Summary
I built Perihelion, a self-contained gravity-survival arcade game.

Concept: A dying star collapses at the center of a circular arena. You pilot a small craft with real inertia/drag physics, harvesting energy shards and dodging gravity-flung debris, while a lethal "event horizon" slowly grows and a "containment field" slowly shrinks around it — squeezing your safe flying band from both sides for as long as you can survive. Score comes from shards (with a combo multiplier) and survival time, with a local high score.

What I built:

src/game/engine.ts — a hand-written Canvas2D game engine: gravity physics, particle system, procedural debris polygons, layered additive-blend rendering (star corona/accretion wisps, glow, shield bubbles, engine trails), screen shake, damage vignette, resize-safe/DPR-aware canvas handling, and a unified pointer-drag "virtual joystick" that works identically for mouse and touch, combined with WASD/arrow keyboard input.
src/game/audio.ts — fully synthesized WebAudio sound (ambient pad, thrust noise bed, chimes, alarms, explosions) with a single master gain node so mute is instant and provable.
src/components/UI.tsx + src/App.tsx — React shell for start/pause/game-over screens, HUD (hull, score, combo, containment gap, danger warnings), and toast notifications, wired to the engine via callbacks; handles tab-blur auto-pause and corrupt-localStorage-safe high score persistence.
